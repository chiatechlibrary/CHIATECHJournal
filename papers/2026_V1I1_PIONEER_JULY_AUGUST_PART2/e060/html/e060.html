The Impact of National Cybersecurity Operations Coordination on Critical Economic Sectors and Economic Growth in Nigeria, 2000-2025: A Policy-Economic Evidence Synthesis and Evaluation Framework

Iboro Tom Idim

Abstract

Background: Nigeria's economic activity increasingly depends on networked services, creating systemic exposure to cyber disruption. Objective: This study examines how national cybersecurity operations coordination can protect critical economic sectors and support resilience while distinguishing defensible evidence from causal claims that available national time series cannot sustain. Methods: A longitudinal policy-economic evidence synthesis maps institutional milestones, economic transmission channels and an evaluation design using Nigerian laws, national coordination and incident-response materials, critical-infrastructure policy, financial-sector frameworks, ITU capability benchmarks and macroeconomic indicators. Results: Nigeria's architecture has moved toward a coordinated model centred on NCCC, ngCERT and sector CSIRTs; the 2024 CNII Order identifies thirteen critical sectors. Conclusion: Coordination is best understood as resilience infrastructure whose value lies in avoided loss, shorter disruption, faster recovery and stronger confidence. A credible growth estimate requires harmonised incident, downtime, response-time, loss and sector-output data; this article provides a reproducible evaluation framework rather than an invented treatment effect.

Abstract

Background: Nigeria's economic activity increasingly depends on digital payments, telecommunications, energy control systems, public-sector platforms and other networked services. This dependence increases productivity and market reach but also creates systemic exposure to cyber incidents that can interrupt critical services, destroy data, impose recovery costs and weaken trust. Objective: This study examines how national cybersecurity operations coordination can protect critical economic sectors and support economic resilience in Nigeria over 2000-2025, while distinguishing defensible evidence from causal claims that the available national time series cannot sustain. Methods: A longitudinal policy-economic evidence synthesis was conducted using Nigeria's cybersecurity laws and strategies, the National Cybersecurity Coordination Centre and ngCERT operational model, the 2024 Critical National Information Infrastructure Order, Central Bank of Nigeria cybersecurity frameworks, International Telecommunication Union capability benchmarks, Nigerian Communications Commission evidence on cybercrime and digital-sector exposure, and World Bank macroeconomic indicators. The study maps institutional milestones, identifies economic transmission channels, and specifies a sector-panel and interrupted-time-series evaluation design for future causal testing. Results: Nigeria's architecture evolved from fragmented sector controls toward a coordinated national model centred on NCCC, ngCERT and sector CSIRTs. The 2024 CNII Order designates thirteen economic sectors for critical protection. ITU's 2024 Global Cybersecurity Index classifies Nigeria in Tier 3, with strongest performance in legal and technical measures and weaker capacity-development and cooperation scores. Earlier Nigerian evidence estimated cybercrime losses at about 0.08% of GDP in 2016, while current ngCERT advisories show persistent phishing, ransomware and exploitation risks to finance, telecommunications, government and other critical services. Conclusion: National cyber operations coordination is best interpreted as resilience infrastructure: its economic value arises from avoided loss, shorter disruption, faster recovery, reduced cross-sector contagion and stronger confidence in digital services. A credible growth estimate requires harmonized incident, downtime, response-time, loss and sector-output data; the paper therefore provides a reproducible evaluation framework rather than an invented treatment effect.

Keywords: cybersecurity economics; National Cybersecurity Coordination Centre; ngCERT; critical national information infrastructure; economic growth; operational resilience; Nigeria

1. Introduction

Nigeria's economy has become more digitally mediated across banking, telecommunications, government services, identity systems, transport, health, energy and commerce. Digitalisation lowers transaction costs and extends service reach, but it also converts cyber availability, integrity and trust into economic inputs. A successful ransomware campaign against a payment processor, a compromise of telecommunications infrastructure, or a disruptive attack on operational technology can therefore move quickly from a technical event to lost transactions, service downtime, recovery expenditure, reputational damage and reduced confidence. The economic problem is not simply the number of attacks; it is the extent to which incidents disrupt productive activity and propagate across interdependent sectors.

Cyber risk has the characteristics of an externality and a coordination problem. Organisations may underinvest when the benefits of their security spending spill over to customers, suppliers and other networks, while attackers exploit the weakest connected entity. The economic literature accordingly treats cybersecurity as a domain of incomplete information, misaligned incentives and interdependence. Recent work also shows that cyber incidents can depress productivity and growth by forcing labour and capital away from productive use and into recovery. A national coordination function can address part of this market failure by sharing threat intelligence, coordinating incident response, setting common protection baselines and reducing the time between local detection and cross-sector action (Abrardi, Comino, & Grassini, 2026; Uguet & Jung, 2026).

Nigeria's institutional architecture has strengthened in stages. The Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) Act 2015 created a statutory basis for national coordination. The National Cybersecurity Policy and Strategy 2021 places the National Cybersecurity Coordination Centre (NCCC) at the centre of implementation and coordination. The Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team (ngCERT) operates as the national incident-response capability under the NCCC. In 2024, the Cybercrimes Act was amended, the Central Bank of Nigeria issued a revised risk-based cybersecurity framework for deposit money banks and payment service banks, and the Designation and Protection of Critical National Information Infrastructure Order formally identified thirteen critical sectors. These instruments transform cybersecurity from a narrow IT-control problem into an issue of economic continuity and national resilience.

The source study proposed an examination of a national cybersecurity operations centre over 2000-2025. For institutional accuracy, this paper uses Nigeria's official terminology - NCCC, ngCERT and the sector CSIRT network - while retaining the underlying research question: whether coordinated national cyber operations can protect critical economic activity and contribute to growth. The paper does not assign a causal coefficient to NCCC because a consistent annual national series on incidents, mean time to detect, mean time to recover, sector downtime and avoided loss is not publicly available for the entire period. Instead, it combines policy history, economic evidence and operational metrics into a transparent framework that can support a later causal evaluation.

2. Cybersecurity, Critical Sectors and the Nigerian Economy

2.1 Digital dependence and economic exposure

The economic importance of cybersecurity rises with digital dependence. World Bank indicators show that Nigeria's GDP reached about US$290.8 billion in 2025 and real GDP growth was approximately 4.0%, while internet use had expanded substantially by the mid-2020s. The Nigerian Communications Commission's sector statistics also show a large telecommunications contribution to GDP over the last decade, although recent figures require caution because the National Bureau of Statistics rebased GDP. These indicators do not measure cybersecurity, but they show why service availability and confidence in digital infrastructure matter to aggregate output.

An older NCC-commissioned study on cybercrime, foreign direct investment and national development reported that the Federal Government estimated the annual cost of cybercrime in 2016 at about 0.08% of GDP, roughly N127 billion at the time. Such figures are necessarily approximate because many incidents are unreported and indirect losses are difficult to observe. They nonetheless illustrate a central economic point: the loss function extends beyond stolen funds to business interruption, remediation costs, intellectual-property loss, reputational damage, weakened investor confidence and reduced trust in online services.

2.2 Critical National Information Infrastructure

The 2024 Designation and Protection of Critical National Information Infrastructure Order links cybersecurity directly to national imperatives, economic development, public health, safety, defence and government operations. It designates information and communications systems in thirteen economic sectors as Critical National Information Infrastructure (CNII). The Order also requires protection planning, information sharing, audits and implementation measures. This creates a legal basis for treating cyber resilience as a system-wide economic policy issue rather than a collection of isolated organisational security programmes.

3. Theoretical Framework: Cyber Resilience as Economic Infrastructure

3.1 Expected-loss economics

Cybersecurity expenditure is economically justified when the expected value of avoided losses and continuity gains exceeds the cost of controls. At sector s and time t, expected cyber loss can be represented as the probability of a material incident multiplied by the combined direct, downtime and spillover consequences:

ECL(s,t) = P(Incident(s,t)) × [DirectLoss(s,t) + DowntimeLoss(s,t) + SpilloverLoss(s,t)]

National coordination can affect both terms. Threat intelligence, common reporting and exercises can reduce the probability that known techniques succeed; coordinated containment, forensics and recovery can reduce loss severity after compromise. The net resilience dividend is therefore the expected baseline loss minus residual loss after coordination and minus the resource cost of operating the national and sector response architecture.

Resilience Dividend = ECL(baseline) - ECL(coordinated) - Coordination Cost

3.2 Public-good and network-externality logic

Cybersecurity is interdependent. An institution that rapidly reports a ransomware indicator may enable dozens of other operators to block the same campaign before compromise. Conversely, an unprotected supplier can transmit risk into multiple critical operators. Information sharing, common minimum controls and sector CSIRTs therefore produce spillovers that private firms may not fully internalise. This public-good logic supports a national coordination layer, provided that it does not displace the responsibility of operators to maintain their own governance, security operations, backups and incident-response capacity.

3.3 Growth and productivity channel

A cybersecurity coordination institution should not be modelled as a direct production factor in the same way as labour or physical capital. Its economic function is closer to risk-adjusted infrastructure. By reducing expected downtime, loss and uncertainty, resilience can protect the effective utilisation of digital capital, support transaction continuity and preserve confidence. A growth equation therefore requires cyber-resilience variables alongside digital intensity and standard macroeconomic controls:

Δln(Yt) = α + β·CyberResilience(t) + γ·DigitalIntensity(t) + δX(t) + ε(t)

Here Y is real output, DigitalIntensity captures the economy's dependence on ICT-enabled activity, and X includes capital formation, labour, inflation, exchange-rate conditions, trade exposure and major shocks. The coefficient β should be interpreted causally only when the resilience measure has a credible source of exogenous variation or a well-specified counterfactual.

5. Method and Evidence Design

5.1 Research design

The study uses a longitudinal policy-economic evidence-synthesis design covering 2000-2025. It is retrospective and observational. It combines documentary analysis of laws, strategies, operational mandates and regulatory frameworks with descriptive macro-digital indicators and an explicit econometric design for future testing. This structure is chosen because a national operations centre cannot be credibly evaluated using a simple before-after GDP comparison: Nigeria's growth over the period was simultaneously affected by oil-price cycles, exchange-rate regimes, population growth, recessions, the COVID-19 shock, inflation, insecurity, telecommunications expansion and multiple financial-sector reforms.

5.3 Measurement strategy

A defensible national evaluation should separate capability, incident performance, economic loss and macroeconomic outcomes. Capability indicators measure whether institutions and controls exist; operational indicators measure whether incidents are detected and resolved more effectively; economic indicators measure avoided disruption and loss; macro outcomes capture whether these gains are large enough to affect sector output or aggregate growth. Collapsing these levels into a single 'cybersecurity score' risks spurious inference.

Table 4. Measurement hierarchy for a national cyber-operations economic evaluation.

5.4 Proposed econometric identification

The preferred empirical design is a sector-by-time panel rather than a single national annual series. Sectors differ in digital intensity, exposure and timing of regulatory implementation, creating potentially useful variation. A fixed-effects specification can compare changes within sectors while controlling for common macro shocks:

Growth(s,t) = αs + λt + β·CyberOpsIntensity(s,t) + θZ(s,t) + ε(s,t)

CyberOpsIntensity should be built from observed operational metrics rather than policy-announcement dummies alone. Where exact implementation dates are available, interrupted-time-series or staggered difference-in-differences designs may be considered, but only after testing pre-trends and accounting for contemporaneous reforms. The 2024 CNII designation is not, by itself, an exogenous treatment because sectors were selected precisely because they were critical. Any evaluation must therefore avoid interpreting designation as random assignment.

5.5 Validity and evidence discipline

The study uses three safeguards. First, it distinguishes legal or institutional commitment from operational performance. Second, it does not infer economic causality from the temporal coincidence of stronger cyber policy and GDP growth. Third, it treats public loss estimates as lower-bound or approximate measures because incident under-reporting, confidential settlements and intangible losses are substantial. These constraints are central to the paper's contribution: a useful economic model must make uncertainty explicit rather than compensate for missing data by inventing precision.

6. Findings from the Evidence Synthesis

6.1 Nigeria has moved toward a coordinated national cyber-operations model

The strongest documentary finding is institutional consolidation. The NCCC is now the national coordinating authority under the Office of the National Security Adviser, while ngCERT provides national incident-response capability and sector CSIRTs form the operational bridge between individual operators and national coordination. The escalation logic published by NCCC is clear: incidents begin at the organisation, move to the sector CSIRT where wider sector impact is assessed, and reach NCCC/ngCERT when they cross sectors or become nationally significant. This reduces ambiguity over responsibility and creates a mechanism for turning one organisation's incident into early warning for others.

6.2 Critical-infrastructure protection is now economically explicit

The 2024 CNII Order materially strengthens the economic framing of cybersecurity. Its objectives explicitly refer to continued operation and to systems that drive economic development, public health, safety, defence and government operations. By naming thirteen sectors, the Order recognises cyber interdependence beyond banking and telecommunications. This matters because national loss from a cyber incident depends on network effects: an energy failure can impair telecommunications; telecommunications disruption can impair payments; payment disruption can propagate into retail, logistics and public services.

6.3 National capability is substantial but uneven

ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024 places Nigeria in Tier 3, 'Establishing'. Nigeria scored 19.52/20 on legal measures and 18.88/20 on technical measures, compared with 16.57 for organisational measures, 13.15 for capacity development and 14.28 for cooperation. The pattern is analytically useful: law and technical structures are relatively strong, while workforce, institutional integration and cooperation remain areas where marginal investment may generate high resilience returns. The index measures commitment and capability, not the number of prevented attacks or the GDP effect of NCCC.

6.4 Threat pressure remains material

ngCERT advisories in 2024-2026 report ransomware, phishing, credential harvesting, exploitation of unpatched systems and attacks against cloud and telecommunications services used by government, finance, education, health and other critical organisations. This persistence means that stronger national capacity should not be evaluated by a fall in raw incident counts alone. Better monitoring can initially increase reported incidents. More meaningful outcomes are severity-adjusted losses, time to detection, time to containment, time to recovery, cross-sector propagation and the proportion of incidents detected before material business disruption.

6.5 Economic contribution is primarily protective

The evidence supports a bounded economic claim. Cyber operations coordination can protect growth by reducing the expected cost of disruption and by preserving trust in digital services; it cannot, by itself, guarantee higher GDP growth. The mechanism is strongest in sectors with high digital intensity and systemic connectivity. The financial system illustrates this logic: the CBN's 2024 framework emphasises governance, risk management, resilience, emerging technologies, metrics, monitoring and compliance because cyber failure can impose financial loss and undermine public confidence. Similar logic applies to power, telecommunications, health and government platforms.

8. Policy Implications

First, Nigeria should treat cyber resilience as productive infrastructure. Budget appraisal for critical-sector cybersecurity should compare control costs with expected avoided loss, downtime and systemic spillover, rather than treating cybersecurity as an overhead detached from service performance. Major digital infrastructure projects should include measurable security, recovery and incident-reporting outcomes from design through operation.

Second, priority should shift from institution-building alone to operational performance. The existence of laws, centres and frameworks is necessary but not sufficient. NCCC and sector regulators should standardise a small set of incident-performance metrics, including severity-normalised detection, containment and recovery times. A national dashboard can report trends without disclosing sensitive technical details.

Third, sector CSIRTs should be strengthened as the main transmission layer between organisation-level security operations and national coordination. Sector teams understand specialised technologies and can distinguish routine incidents from systemic events. Regular exercises should test cross-sector dependencies such as power-telecommunications-payments and identity-government-service chains.

Fourth, capacity development and cooperation deserve particular emphasis. Nigeria's ITU profile shows these pillars lagging legal and technical measures. Investment in analysts, digital forensics, threat-intelligence practice, incident command, industrial-control-system security and executive cyber-risk governance can therefore complement existing legal authority. Universities and professional bodies should be integrated into the training pipeline.

Fifth, economic research should be embedded into incident-response governance. A standard post-incident economic-impact form should capture direct financial loss, service-hours lost, staff recovery time, customer impact, third-party propagation and restoration cost. These data would allow economists to move from broad national loss claims to sector-specific estimates of resilience dividends.

9. Limitations and Research Priorities

This paper does not estimate a causal GDP effect for NCCC or ngCERT. The national coordination architecture emerged gradually, its intensity is not observed consistently from 2000, and macroeconomic outcomes are affected by numerous confounders. Public incident data are incomplete and reporting improves as capability matures, creating measurement bias. Telecommunications contribution to GDP is also affected by national-account rebasing, so longitudinal comparisons require harmonised NBS series.

Future research should construct a secure sector panel using regulator and operator records. The strongest design would combine quarterly sector output with incident severity, MTTD, MTTR, downtime, cybersecurity spending, digital intensity and implementation dates of sector controls. Synthetic-control, interrupted-time-series or difference-in-differences methods could then be considered where assumptions are defensible. Case studies of major incidents should complement econometrics because cross-sector propagation and recovery decisions are not fully captured by aggregate data.

10. Conclusion

Nigeria's cybersecurity architecture has evolved from dispersed sector controls toward a nationally coordinated model centred on NCCC, ngCERT and sector CSIRTs. The 2024 CNII Order makes the economic stakes explicit by designating thirteen sectors whose digital systems are critical to national development, public safety and government operations. Evidence from national reports, sector regulation, ITU capability benchmarks and current threat advisories supports the proposition that cyber operations coordination can protect economic activity by reducing disruption, loss and systemic contagion.

The appropriate economic claim is therefore a resilience claim, not an automatic growth claim. A national coordination centre creates value when it helps operators detect earlier, contain faster, recover more reliably and warn others before attacks spread. Measuring that value requires incident-level and sector-level operational data linked to economic outcomes. The evaluation framework and scorecard developed in this article provide a route from policy commitment to measurable economic evidence without inventing a causal effect that the current public data cannot support.
