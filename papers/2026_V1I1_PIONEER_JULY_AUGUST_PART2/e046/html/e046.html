Abstract

Background: A 2016 engineering project at the Federal University of Agriculture Makurdi designed and implemented a pulse-width-modulated buck converter to step 24 V DC down to a regulated 19 V output. Objective: This article converts that archived project into a compact, publication-ready engineering paper, reconstructing the power-stage mathematics, voltage-mode feedback loop and Type-III compensation while preserving the documented prototype and simulation evidence. Methods: The archived specifications were audited and re-expressed using continuous-conduction-mode (CCM) equations, small-signal control-to-output modeling, K-factor pole-zero placement and analytical ripple calculations. The source specifies 24 V input, 19 V rated output, 3.5 A, 66.5 W, 30 kHz switching, L = 251 µH, C = 15 µF, capacitor ESR = 10 mΩ and inductor DCR = 2 mΩ. Results: The ideal duty ratio is 0.7917. At the 3.5 A design point the equivalent rated load is 5.43 Ω, while the archived simulation schematic separately uses a nominal 5 Ω test load. The reconstructed inductor ripple is approximately 0.526 A peak-to-peak (15.0% of rated output current), with an analytical output-ripple estimate of about 0.151 V peak-to-peak when capacitive and ESR terms are combined. The LC double-pole frequency is approximately 2.594 kHz and the capacitor-ESR zero approximately 1.061 MHz. The documented Type-III network (R1 = 2.2 kΩ, R2 = 5.9 kΩ, R3 = 38.8 Ω, C1 = 34.2 nF, C2 = 0.6 nF, C3 = 90 nF) is consistent with a voltage-mode compensation strategy that places two zeros around the LC resonance and high-frequency poles above the crossover region. Conclusion: The archived design is technically coherent at its rated point and demonstrates the engineering value of PWM, CCM energy transfer and Type-III loop shaping. The article also identifies legacy reporting inconsistencies that should not be interpreted as new measurements, notably the source wording that labels 19 V and 3.5 A as ripple values.

1. Introduction

Switch-mode DC-DC converters regulate voltage by transferring energy through controlled switching rather than dissipating the excess voltage as heat. In the buck topology, a high-side switch, freewheeling path, inductor and output capacitor form a second-order power stage whose average output is controlled primarily through duty ratio. Modern design guidance continues to treat the inductor-current ripple, output-capacitor ESR, switching frequency and control-loop bandwidth as coupled design variables (Hauke, 2015; Analog Devices, n.d.-a). The archived project that forms the evidence base for this article was completed in April 2016 by Chia S. Kenneth under the supervision of Goshwe N. Y. It targeted a 24 V-to-19 V PWM converter, used voltage feedback and Type-III compensation, and reported both simulation and Vero-board implementation.

Figure 1. Reconstructed system architecture of the archived PWM buck converter. The original project describes a voltage-feedback loop in which the measured output is compared with a 19 V reference, compensated, converted to PWM by a sawtooth comparator and applied to the MOSFET through a gate driver.

The contribution of e046 is not to relabel a 2016 student project as a new 2026 experiment. The project uses a single outer voltage loop. The output-voltage error is amplified and shaped by the compensator; the resulting control voltage is compared with a fixed-frequency sawtooth to generate the gate PWM. This is the conventional voltage-mode architecture described in contemporary control-loop guidance, in which the LC double pole introduces up to −180° phase shift and usually motivates Type-III compensation when a wider bandwidth is desired (Analog Devices, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

3.2 Power, load and switch stress

For the ideal design point, the MOSFET must conduct approximately the inductor current during D·Ts and block approximately Vin during OFF-time; the freewheeling diode carries the inductor current during (1−D)·Ts. Practical device selection therefore requires voltage margin above 24 V, current margin above the reconstructed peak current, low conduction loss and switching performance compatible with 30 kHz. The archived schematic identifies Q1 as a MOSFET and lists a 3 A diode selection in the design narrative, while another component table identifies an IN4141-type device. That naming inconsistency is retained as a source limitation; e046 does not substitute a specific modern part number without a new hardware audit.

4. Analytical Results and Comparison with Archived Evidence

4.1 Rated-point ripple and CCM behavior

Figure 7. Analytical rated-point ripple. These are reconstructed theoretical waveforms from the documented L, C, ESR, fs, Vin and Vo; they are not digitized measurements from the archived MATLAB figure.

The reconstructed inductor ripple of 0.526 A p-p is exactly 15.0% of the rated 3.5 A current, closely matching the project design target of 15% current ripple. The corresponding output-voltage ripple estimate is about 0.151 V p-p, or 0.80% of the 19 V output. This distinction matters because the archived report states that the “measured ripple” values are 19 V and 3.5 A; those numbers coincide with the output-voltage and rated-current levels rather than plausible ripple amplitudes. e046 therefore treats that wording as a legacy reporting error and does not reproduce it as a ripple measurement.

4.3 Archived simulation and prototype evidence

The source report documents a full Proteus-style schematic, a MATLAB/Simulink converter model, PWM waveforms, output-voltage/load-current plots and implementation first on a breadboard and then on Vero board. It concludes that the converter produced the 19 V target from 24 V at 30 kHz and reports efficiency above 81% together with a 0.5 s settling-time statement. However, the archived pages available for this publication do not provide a traceable efficiency table, calibrated oscilloscope scales or a complete load sweep from which those two performance claims can be independently recomputed. Accordingly, they are retained as archived project observations rather than elevated to newly verified 2026 measurements.

5. Discussion

5.1 What the design demonstrates

First, the power-stage sizing is internally coherent at the central design point: the 79.17% duty ratio follows directly from the target voltage ratio, and the 251 µH inductor produces almost exactly the intended 15% current ripple at 30 kHz. Second, the 15 µF capacitor with 10 mΩ ESR gives a sub-1% first-order output-ripple estimate at rated current, illustrating how capacitor selection and ESR jointly shape both ripple and loop dynamics. Third, the LC resonance near 2.6 kHz is close enough to the intended control bandwidth that simple integral compensation would be inadequate for a fast voltage-mode loop; Type III is therefore a technically defensible choice. This agrees with modern guidance that voltage-mode buck converters frequently require two compensating zeros around the LC resonance and high-frequency poles to preserve phase margin while attenuating switching noise (Analog Devices, n.d.-a; Texas Instruments, 2010).

5.3 Engineering upgrades for a contemporary re-build

A new build should retain the topology-learning value of the project while improving measurement traceability and protection. Recommended upgrades are: (i) use a modern low-RDS(on) MOSFET and a diode/synchronous rectifier with documented voltage/current margin; (ii) add input and output decoupling close to the switching loop; (iii) measure loop gain with a frequency-response analyzer or injection method rather than relying only on simulation; (iv) record output ripple with bandwidth-limited differential probing and clearly stated probe ground technique; (v) log input power and output power simultaneously to produce an efficiency curve versus load; (vi) quantify startup overshoot and settling time against a defined tolerance band; and (vii) use current limiting, over-voltage protection, thermal sensing and controlled dead time where appropriate. Modern vendor guidance also recommends verifying the calculated loop against time-domain load transients and component tolerances rather than treating a nominal Bode plot as sufficient by itself (Analog Devices, n.d.-b).

6. Conclusion

The 24 V-to-19 V PWM buck converter project is a defensible foundation for a technical journal article when its historical evidence and its modern analytical reconstruction are clearly separated. The core design is mathematically consistent: D ≈ 0.7917, Pout = 66.5 W at 3.5 A, the 251 µH inductor yields about 0.526 A p-p ripple at 30 kHz, and the LC resonance is approximately 2.594 kHz. The documented Type-III network and K-factor of about 57.7 are consistent with the need for substantial phase boost in voltage-mode control. The original project further documents MATLAB/Simulink and Proteus-based development and a Vero-board implementation that achieved the 19 V target. The most important publication improvement in e046 is evidentiary discipline: analytical results are explicitly labeled, legacy inconsistencies are exposed rather than hidden, and no new bench precision is invented from uncalibrated historical plots.
