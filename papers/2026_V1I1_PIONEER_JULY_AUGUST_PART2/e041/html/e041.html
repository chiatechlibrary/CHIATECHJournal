Security Challenges and Principals' Management Strategies in North-Central Nigerian Secondary Schools

Wilfred Ogbu

Department of Educational Management, Faculty of Education, University of Abuja, Abuja, Nigeria

Correspondence: ogbuwilfred212@gmail.com

Abstract

Background: Security threats can disrupt attendance, teaching, learning and school administration, while principals are expected to coordinate preventive, protective, detective and response functions within constrained local environments. Objective: This study examined prevalent security challenges in public senior secondary schools in three North-Central Nigerian states and assessed the effectiveness of principals' security-management strategies. Methods: The source manuscript reports a descriptive correlational survey of 45 schools drawn from a population of 894 public senior secondary schools in Benue, Kogi and Nasarawa. Respondents comprised 45 principals, 375 teachers and 384 students. Three four-point questionnaires were used, with reported reliability coefficients of 0.73–0.79. Descriptive statistics and ANOVA were reported at the 0.05 level. Results: The overall security-challenge mean was 2.80 (SD=0.86). Violence (M=3.06), kidnapping/abduction (M=3.04) and communal clashes (M=2.93) were the highest-rated threats. State-level challenge means were Nasarawa 2.93, Kogi 2.65 and Benue 2.57, with a reported significant difference (F=3.689, p=.033). Principals were rated effective in safety programmes (M=2.71), asset protection (M=2.67) and detective strategies (M=2.60), but ineffective in threat prevention (M=2.43) and emergency response (M=2.20). State differences were reported only for asset-protection strategy (F=3.293, p=.038). Conclusion: The evidence indicates a high-threat environment combined with a management gap in prevention and emergency response. The publication update preserves the source statistics without re-estimation and situates the findings within Nigeria's current safe-schools policy framework.

1. Introduction

School security is both a protection obligation and a condition for effective educational management. When violence, kidnapping, communal conflict, harassment, theft, cult-related activity or environmental hazards enter the school environment, the consequences can extend beyond immediate physical harm to attendance, staff deployment, concentration, school-community trust and continuity of instruction. In conflict-exposed settings, principals are therefore required to do more than administer routine school operations: they must coordinate risk assessment, prevention, communication, protection, detection and emergency response with actors whose authority and resources often sit outside the school.

The source study supplied for this journal update focuses on public senior secondary schools in Benue, Kogi and Nasarawa States. It was designed around three practical concerns: the prevalence of security challenges, the effectiveness of principals' management strategies and variation across states. This emphasis remains policy-relevant. Nigeria's National Policy on Safety, Security and Violence-Free Schools frames safety as a shared responsibility requiring prevention, preparedness, protection and response, while the National Plan on Financing Safe Schools (2023–2026) links school protection to security-resilient communities, strengthened response capabilities and coordinated implementation. UNICEF's monitoring of the Minimum Standards for Safe Schools has subsequently shown that implementation gaps remain substantial, particularly in early warning and basic security infrastructure.

The significance of the present evidence lies in the contrast between threat intensity and administrative readiness. A school may have guards, gates or awareness programmes and still remain vulnerable if warning systems, threat prevention, medical readiness, evacuation protocols, referral pathways and external response arrangements are weak. The study's five strategy domains—safety programmes, asset protection, threat prevention, detective strategies and emergency response—therefore provide a useful administrative lens for distinguishing visible protection from full-spectrum preparedness.

1.1 Objectives and research focus

The source article examined: (1) the prevalent security challenges in North-Central Nigerian secondary schools; (2) the effectiveness of principals' security-management strategies; and (3) state-level variations in security challenges and management approaches. e041 retains those aims. Current policy and recent literature are added only to interpret the reported findings; they are not used to alter the original sample, coefficients, means, standard deviations, F statistics or p values.

2. Conceptual and Policy Framework

For educational administration, school security can be understood as a management system rather than a single intervention. Prevention reduces exposure before an incident; protection limits access and vulnerability; detection identifies threats and abnormal conditions; response coordinates immediate action; and recovery restores learning, psychosocial stability and institutional functioning. These functions require leadership, resource allocation, communication and collaboration with security agencies, parents and host communities. The source manuscript uses closely related categories and shows that performance can be uneven across them.

Nigeria's current policy architecture supports this system view. The 2021 national school-safety policy establishes a coordinated framework for safe and violence-free schools. The 2023–2026 financing plan adopts a whole-society approach and provides for security response and coordination capacity. UNICEF's 2024 monitoring report, covering implementation of the Minimum Standards for Safe Schools, found major gaps in early-warning and infrastructure performance. The 2025 Nigeria case study on implementing the Safe Schools Declaration further places implementation, coordination and accountability at the centre of protecting education from attack. These policy sources are publication-update context; they are not part of the original statistical analysis.

3. Methodology and Evidence Provenance

3.1 Research design, population and sample

The source manuscript reports a descriptive correlational survey design. The study population comprised 894 public senior secondary schools across Benue, Kogi and Nasarawa States. Multistage sampling produced 45 schools, described in the source as approximately 5% of the school population. Respondents were 45 principals, 375 teachers and 384 students.

3.2 Instruments and analysis

Three instruments are named in the source: the Prevalent Security Challenges Questionnaire (PSCQ) for principals; the Principals' Security Management Strategy and Conduciveness of Teaching Environment Questionnaire (PSMSCTEQ) for teachers; and the Conduciveness of the Learning Environment Questionnaire (CLEQ) for students. Each used a four-point Likert response format. Reliability coefficients are reported as ranging from 0.73 to 0.79. The methods section states that means, standard deviations, ANOVA and multiple regression were used at a 0.05 significance level.

Evidence boundary: The accessible results for this specific article report descriptive statistics and ANOVA tables, but do not provide regression coefficients, model summaries or degrees of freedom. e041 therefore does not create or infer missing regression results. Likewise, no row-level dataset, codebook, analysis syntax, ethics approval identifier or consent documentation was available for independent verification in the material used for the publication update.

3.3 Source integrity and publication-update rules

The article associated by the author/editor with Zenodo record 17834595 is treated as the empirical source. Numerical values are reproduced as reported. One ANOVA row for safety-program strategy gives F=3.689 and p=.584 without degrees of freedom; that pair is retained verbatim rather than recalculated. 5. Discussion

5.1 A multi-threat school environment

The reported overall threat level and the fact that every listed challenge was classified as high point to a broad security burden rather than a single-incident problem. Violence, kidnapping and communal clashes led the ranking, but school leaders also faced harassment, arson, cult-related activity, cybercrime, flooding, intimidation, drug abuse, burglary and bullying. This breadth matters administratively because a protection model optimized only for external intrusion cannot address internal violence, psychosocial threats, digital risk or environmental hazards.

The state difference reinforces the need for risk-sensitive planning. Nasarawa had the highest source mean, but the absence of post-hoc comparisons means the study does not establish which state pairs differ statistically. The practical implication is nevertheless compatible with Nigeria's current safe-schools approach: national minimum standards require local implementation that responds to the threat profile, infrastructure and response capacity of each school and community rather than assuming uniform exposure.

5.2 Strength in visible protection, weakness in prevention and response

The management pattern is especially important. Safety programmes, asset protection and detective strategies were rated effective, while threat prevention and emergency response were not. This suggests an asymmetry between measures that are relatively visible—guards, gadgets, awareness and detection—and the less visible organizational work required before and after a crisis: threat assessment, early warning, escalation, evacuation, first aid, medical referral, reunification, psychosocial support and continuity planning.

The item-level results support that interpretation. Security guards and security gadgets ranked strongly, whereas bell signals and medical kits were weak. Current Nigerian monitoring evidence identifies related implementation gaps: UNICEF's 2024 report found that early-warning systems were present in only a minority of assessed schools and that basic safety infrastructure remained weak in many settings. The source findings therefore fit a wider implementation problem in which formal policy and visible security inputs may exist without complete preparedness capability.

5.3 Leadership, financing and partnerships

Principals cannot close systemic security gaps alone. Threat prevention and emergency response depend on budget authority, infrastructure, training, intelligence, emergency services and inter-agency coordination. Nigeria's financing plan explicitly adopts a whole-society approach involving federal, state and local governments, security agencies, communities, businesses, donors and other partners. For school leadership, this means that a defensible security plan should specify who owns each risk, who receives alerts, how external responders are contacted, which resources are maintained, how drills are evaluated and how learning resumes after an incident.

Recent North-Central research also reports that insecurity affects monitoring, evaluation and teaching-learning processes and calls for technology-supported quality control and wider implementation of the Safe School Initiative. The convergence is useful but should not be overstated: both studies are survey-based and do not establish the causal effect of any single security intervention. What they do support is the administrative priority of integrating school-security planning with educational quality and continuity.

6. Publication-Update Limitations and Reproducibility

The study is based on a three-state sample and should not be generalized to all North-Central states without qualification. Its survey design relies on respondents' ratings, and the source does not provide item wording, respondent-response rates, validity statistics beyond the stated reliability range, degrees of freedom for the ANOVAs, post-hoc tests, or full regression output despite listing multiple regression in the analysis plan. It also does not provide a row-level dataset or analysis syntax in the material used for e041.

The accessible source manuscript's reference section is incomplete, ending with a placeholder rather than a full bibliography. This update therefore avoids reconstructing unverified references. The References section below contains the source record plus independently verifiable policy and recent empirical materials used for the publication-update discussion. Future republication or replication would be strengthened by archiving the questionnaires, scoring rules, anonymized data, codebook, statistical output and ethics/consent documentation alongside the article.

7. Conclusion and Recommendations

The source evidence portrays North-Central public secondary schools as operating within a high and diverse security-risk environment. Violence, kidnapping and communal clashes were the strongest reported threats, while security conditions differed significantly across the three sampled states. Principals were comparatively stronger in safety programmes, asset protection and detective strategies than in threat prevention and emergency response. The central administrative implication is therefore not simply to add more visible security hardware, but to build a complete prevention-to-recovery system around school leadership.

1. State education authorities should require school-specific threat registers and annual security plans aligned with national safe-school standards and local risk profiles.

2. Principal-training programmes should prioritize threat assessment, early warning, emergency command, evacuation, first aid, reunification, continuity planning and post-incident review in addition to routine safety awareness.

3. School management should test emergency communication systems through scheduled drills and document response times, equipment readiness, lessons learned and corrective actions.

4. Government and financing partners should protect budget lines for perimeter safety, lighting, communication, medical readiness, surveillance where justified, maintenance and trained personnel, rather than funding equipment without operating support.

5. Schools, parents and host communities should establish structured reporting and information-sharing channels linked to security agencies and emergency services, with clear escalation responsibilities.

6. Student and staff support systems should include confidential reporting, safeguarding referral and trauma-informed psychosocial support for people affected by security incidents.

7. Researchers and education authorities should replicate the study across all North-Central states using transparent sampling, validated instruments, complete ANOVA/regression output, post-hoc tests where required and a shareable anonymized replication package.
