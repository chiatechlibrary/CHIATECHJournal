TECHNOLOGY · CONCEPTUAL PRACTICE REVIEW AND DESIGN FRAMEWORK

Prompting for Human-Centred Output: The HUMAN Framework for Credible, Context-Aware Generative AI Writing

CHIA Shiaondo Kenneth

CHIATECH Solutions and Resources Ltd, Abuja, Nigeria

ORCID: 0009-0009-6434-4586 · Correspondence: chiakenneth01@gmail.com

Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence can accelerate writing, yet a fluent draft may remain generic, unsupported or misaligned with its reader. This conceptual practice review develops the HUMAN framework for credible and context-aware AI-assisted writing: Human intent and evidence; User and audience alignment; Message architecture; Authentic detail and accountability; and Natural revision and judgement. An integrative synthesis connected research on prompt design, human-AI co-writing, iterative refinement, hallucination and educational AI governance with the practical requirements of academic, professional and public communication. The synthesis shows that quality is created both upstream, by supplying verified source material and operational constraints, and downstream, through targeted human review. Surface instructions to 'sound human' cannot substitute for evidence, a real audience, a section-level purpose and accountable verification. The paper provides a nine-part prompt architecture, an editorial rubric and adoption controls covering privacy, attribution, bias, confidentiality and disclosure. The framework does not claim to detect AI authorship or to legitimise impersonation and detector evasion. It positions generative AI as a bounded drafting technology whose output becomes publishable only through human selection, verification and approval. Experimental validation across models, genres, languages and levels of writer expertise remains necessary.

Keywords: prompt engineering; generative AI; human-AI collaboration; editorial quality; source grounding; responsible AI

1. Introduction

Generative AI has changed the speed and cost of drafting. In a controlled experiment involving common professional writing tasks, access to a generative system reduced completion time and increased average quality ratings, with larger gains for weaker initial performers (Noy & Zhang, 2023). Speed, however, is not equivalent to epistemic quality. A fluent paragraph can contain invented facts, generic examples, blurred attribution or conclusions that exceed the evidence. Writing technologies therefore need a design standard that keeps human purpose, knowledge and accountability visible.

Research on human-AI co-writing shows an interactive process: writers request suggestions, accept, reject and reshape them as their intentions evolve (Lee et al., 2022). Higher levels of task scaffolding can improve performance for some writers, but can also reduce felt ownership if the system takes too much control (Dhillon et al., 2024). The practical implication is that an effective framework must support the writer without treating generated prose as an autonomous author.

This paper develops the HUMAN framework and translates it into prompt and editorial controls. 'Human-centred' means oriented towards a real reader, grounded in supplied evidence and context, and approved through responsible human judgement. It does not mean disguising AI use, imitating a living writer, fabricating experience or attempting to defeat AI-detection tools.

2. Integrative Design-Synthesis Method

The framework was developed through integrative design synthesis rather than a systematic review or experiment. Peer-reviewed research was selected on prompt patterns, non-expert prompting behaviour, co-writing, scaffolded interaction, productivity, creativity and hallucination. UNESCO guidance supplied governance principles for human agency, privacy, inclusion and age-appropriate educational use. Concepts were coded by the function they perform in writing: grounding, audience alignment, architecture, authenticity, verification and revision.

Related functions were clustered into five dimensions and translated into three practical objects: an instruction that can be included in a prompt, an observable quality criterion, and an editorial action when that criterion fails. The framework was then stress-tested conceptually against four tasks—an academic explanation, a policy brief, a teaching note and a customer-facing article—to ensure that each dimension could be expressed without relying on model-specific commands.

3. Why Generic AI Writing Occurs

3.1 Under-specified tasks

A prompt such as 'write a professional article about digital education' gives a topic but not a decision, evidence base, audience, context, exclusions or acceptance test. Non-expert users often struggle to translate intentions into robust prompts and to diagnose failure after an unsatisfactory response (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023). The model then fills missing structure with statistically common patterns: broad openings, familiar lists and generic conclusions.

3.2 Fluency without verification

Language models generate likely continuations; they do not independently establish that a cited paper exists or that a local observation occurred. Hallucination research shows that confident false statements can arise from data, training and inference conditions (Ji et al., 2023). A prompt that requests references without supplying verified sources invites citation fabrication. A request for a personal story invites false provenance unless the writer provides a genuine account or explicitly asks for a hypothetical example.

3.3 Convergence of ideas and voice

Generative assistance can improve the judged creativity of individual outputs while reducing diversity across a collection of outputs (Doshi & Hauser, 2024). Writers therefore need to contribute distinctive questions, local evidence, experience, examples and judgements. Style variation alone is insufficient: a text may vary sentence length and still be conceptually empty.

4.1 Human intent and evidence

The source pack should be separated from the instruction. It may contain results, verified policy provisions, meeting notes, product specifications, quotations with permissions or the writer's genuine examples. The prompt should identify which materials are authoritative, prohibit unsupported additions and require missing evidence to be flagged. In academic work, the model may help organise a supplied result table; it must not invent participants, measurements, significance tests or citations.

4.2 User and audience alignment

An audience definition should include knowledge, stakes, reading conditions and action. 'Write for secondary-school principals who understand school administration but not machine learning; they need to decide whether to permit AI-assisted homework after a ten-minute read' is operational. It guides explanation depth and determines whether examples should be pedagogical, legal or technical.

4.3 Message architecture

Strong prompts assign functions: open with the decision and consequence; establish verified context; explain the mechanism; compare options; state limitations; and finish with a proportionate action. Explicit architecture reduces repetition and prevents the title from being restated as an introduction. It also makes the draft easier to audit because every paragraph can be tested against its intended job.

4.4 Authentic detail and accountability

Authenticity comes from actual evidence and bounded voice, not from a list of banned phrases. A writer may supply a short sample of their own approved writing and ask the system to preserve general characteristics such as directness and formality while avoiding copied phrases. A hypothetical case must be labelled. A quotation must be checked against the opened source. Confidential and personal data should not be entered without authority and adequate safeguards.

4.5 Natural revision and judgement

Revision should target named defects: 'remove two unsupported claims, preserve the verified figures, reduce repetition and explain the counterargument in 120 words.' Iterative refinement can improve outputs when the feedback criteria are explicit (Madaan et al., 2023). The last decision remains human: what the text means, whether the evidence is sufficient, whether disclosure is required and whether the output should be published.

The architecture is modular. A simple email may need only purpose, audience, facts and a review check. A journal article requires the complete set. More words do not automatically make a prompt stronger; the goal is to expose the distinctions that matter for acceptance.

6. Editorial Rubric and Governance

A ten-criterion rubric can score purpose fulfilment, audience fit, source fidelity, factual accuracy, reasoning coherence, specificity, voice consistency, concision, accessibility and ethical/provenance compliance. Each criterion may be scored from 0 (harmful or absent) to 4 (publication-ready). A total score must not compensate for a critical failure: source fidelity, factual accountability, privacy or deceptive authorship below an acceptable threshold should block release.

Record the tool, version or access date when required by the publication policy.

Disclose substantive use in drafting, analysis, coding or image production; never list an AI tool as an author.

Keep confidential manuscripts, personal data and restricted information out of unapproved services.

Test for cultural and linguistic exclusion, harmful stereotypes and one-sided framing.

Retain the source pack, human edits and approval record for high-stakes public communication.

UNESCO's guidance for generative AI in education and research emphasises human agency, privacy, inclusion, age appropriateness and institutional validation. These principles generalise beyond schools: an organisation should adopt a tool only after evaluating the task, users, data flows, likely harms, accessibility and mechanisms for correction.

7. Limitations and Validation Agenda

The HUMAN framework is a conceptual synthesis, not a validated causal model. Prompt effectiveness varies by system, language, genre and user expertise. Longer instructions can conflict, and a model may ignore even a clear constraint. Human reviewers can also miss errors or over-trust fluent prose. Controlled studies should compare the full framework with shorter baselines using blinded ratings, factual-error counts, editing time, writer ownership and reader outcomes.

Research in Nigerian and other multilingual contexts should examine whether source-rich prompting improves local relevance without narrowing language diversity. Evaluation should include low-bandwidth workflows, accessibility, education levels and the cost of verification. The framework should be revised when evidence shows that a component is ineffective or burdensome.

8. Conclusion

Credible AI-assisted writing is not produced by asking a model to appear human. It begins with human intent and verified evidence, defines a real user, gives each section a purpose, supplies authentic detail and ends with accountable human judgement. The HUMAN framework converts those principles into a prompt architecture and editorial gate. Used transparently, generative AI can support drafting and revision; it cannot assume authorship, establish truth or approve publication.
